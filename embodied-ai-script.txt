# Embodied AI — speaking script

For the 36-slide deck. Roughly 33 minutes at a normal pace.
Slides 1, 4, 5, 6, 9, 12, 18, 22 and 28 are full-page infographics — the wording below
matches what is printed on them.
Text in *italics* is stage direction, not something to say out loud.
A 15-minute cut is listed at the end.

---
## Audience and thesis

**Who is in the room:** robotics engineers whose training predates the learning-based era.
They know
mechanisms, control and classical perception cold. What they have not tracked is the last
decade of learning-based robotics: what a pretrained policy is, how simulation became a
training tool, what changed in the workflow. Do not re-teach kinematics; do explain the
model vocabulary once each.

**The argument you are making:** robot development has changed shape — task knowledge
moved from hand-written code into trained weights — and because hardware is commoditising,
the brain is now where the value and the difficulty sit. Generative AI is loud, and
robotics people are being pulled into LLM and agent work where none of their training
counts; the better move is to point those same model skills at a body. Slides 4 to 6 set
this up, slide 36 says it outright, and slide 39 lands it.

**Say the qualifier out loud, early and once.** This is not "ignore generative AI." A VLA
*is* a generative model — they have to learn transformers, fine-tuning and flow matching
regardless. The claim is about where to point those skills, not whether to acquire them.
If you skip this, the sharpest person in the room will raise it and the talk loses its
footing.

**The second qualifier belongs on slide 33.** Embodied AI is raising far more than it
sells, and reliable unattended autonomy is forecast around 2028–2030. Do not hide that to
make the call to action cleaner. Use it: the gap between capital and revenue is exactly
the window where preparation is cheap and a scarce skill is worth the most. And say
plainly that the preparation is durable either way — simulation, evaluation and control
skills do not become worthless if the timeline slips.

**Spend your time on** slides 4–6 (the workflow change, why it is worth it, the commodity
argument), 22 (VLA), 24 (the build pipeline), 31 (form factors), 32 (frontiers), 35
(evidence ladder) and 36–39 (the call).

**Where to be brief:** slides 2–3 are framing, not information. The company
and country block, 22–26, is orientation — read the group labels, not the names.

**Register.** Hz, DoF, IK, RRT, DDS and QoS need no definition. Define VLA, flow matching,
domain randomisation and cross-embodiment once each.

## 1 — Cover

*Leave this up while people settle. Don't read the panels aloud.*

"Everyone here has used AI that writes. Most of you have used AI that draws, or codes,
or summarises. Today I want to talk about the version that has to pick something up.

That's embodied AI. The picture on screen is the whole talk in one line: five waves of
AI, ending at machines that act on the physical world. I'll cover why that last panel
is so much harder than the ones before it, who is actually building it, and whether it
is really where the field is going — or just where the money is going."

---

## 2 — Six eras, and what each one unlocked

*Historical on-ramp. Read the six era names and the punchline; let people read the rest.*

"Before the technical part, a century in six eras.

The word arrives in 1920, in a Czech play — Čapek coined it from *robota*, forced
labour. 'Does our work for us' was in the name before any machine existed.

1954 to the seventies, the arm: Unimate at a GM plant. One motion, repeated, in a jig
built so the part is always in exactly the same place.

Now the era I want you to notice. 1966 to '72, SRI's Shakey. It planned multi-step
tasks. It could reason. And it still could not pick up a mug.

Then sense-and-react — subsumption, then SLAM, robots that cope with a world they never
modelled. Then autonomy at scale: the DARPA Grand Challenge, ROS, and deep learning
finally cracking vision in 2012.

And now, instructable robots: models that arrive already knowing what the world contains,
which you talk to instead of program.

The line at the bottom is the whole story. Task planning got a body in 1972. Seeing and handling took another forty years."

---

## 3 — Eight techniques, still all in use

*Don't read all eight. Name three or four and land the bottom line.*

"And here is what actually moved it — every one of these still running underneath a
modern robot.

Feedback control from the fifties: PID holding a joint where you put it. Teach and
repeat from the sixties: record a trajectory, play it back, exact and cheap and helpless
if anything moves.

Planning and search in the seventies through nineties — STRIPS and A*, later RRT.
Probabilistic estimation, Kalman filters and SLAM, so the robot knows where it is
without being told. ROS in 2007 so nobody rewrites a lidar driver again.

Then deep learning for vision in 2012, learned control from 2013, and simulation at GPU
scale from 2017 — training data without buying hardware.

And here is the connection to everything after this slide. Embodied AI is the ninth
layer, not a replacement for the other eight. PID still holds the joint. SLAM still says
where the robot is. ROS still moves the messages. The new model only decides what to do
next.

If you take that seriously, a lot of the hype reads differently — nobody has replaced
robotics. They have added a decision-maker on top of it."

---

## 4 — How a robot programme runs now

*The orientation slide for this audience. Give it time.*

"Here is the change in one picture, and it is the reason for the rest of the talk.

The left column is how most of us were taught. Define the task, design the fixture so the
part is always in the same place, model the kinematics, write a perception pipeline per
object, hand-code the state machine, tune the gains, validate deterministically. And when
the part changes, you go back to the fixture.

The right column is how a programme runs now. Define the task and what counts as success.
Build the evaluation harness — second, not last. Collect demonstrations by teleoperating
the robot. Fine-tune a pretrained policy on them. Evaluate in simulation, then hardware in
the loop. Deploy with the classical controller underneath as fallback. And the failures
become the next dataset.

Notice what did not change. Same actuators, same controllers, same safety layer. What
moved is where the task knowledge lives: it used to sit in code that an engineer wrote,
and now it sits in weights that data produced.

Everything else in this deck follows from that one sentence."

---

## 5 — Hardware is standardising. The brain is not.

*The 'why the brain' argument, and now also the slide that carries the cost of the trade.
This is the one to defend if someone pushes back.*

"And here is why I think this is where our attention should go.

Hardware is standardising. A Unitree G1 starts near thirteen and a half thousand dollars.
Actuators, IMUs, depth cameras, onboard compute — all off the shelf, with Chinese
manufacturing pushing volume up and price down at once. A mechanism that would have taken
two years and a PhD in 2005 can now be bought — and your competitor can buy the same one.

Which means the mechanism is a weaker moat than it was. Actuators and sensing still
differentiate — far less than when you built them yourself. What is left to differentiate on is what the machine can be told
to do, how it copes with a scene nobody staged, and how rarely a human has to step in. Increasingly those
differences are expressed in the policy and the data, not only in the mechanism.

So the scarce assets are the demonstration data, the trained policy, and an evaluation
protocol you can defend. None of those can be bought.

One honest qualifier: a purpose-built machine still beats a general one at a single task,
and it always will. The brain is not what wins the first task. It is what buys you the
second one without a second engineering programme.

And be honest about the price, because this room will think of it anyway. You give up
verifiability — you can no longer read the code and say what the machine will do. You take
on compute and latency onboard. And you take on data: the policy has to be fed with
demonstrations somebody produced. You give up predicting exactly what the machine will do,
and you get a machine whose cost stops scaling with the number of tasks.

That is the argument of the whole talk. The hard, valuable, unbought part of a robot
programme has moved from the mechanism to the intelligence — and the people best placed to
build that intelligence are the ones who already understand the mechanism."

---

## 6 — A chatbot runs in a line. A body runs in a loop.

*Point at the laptop first. The two panels are asking the same question.*

"Here is the one idea everything else follows from — and notice that both sides of this
slide are answering the same question.

On the left, a text model. Someone asks it how a robot should pick up and carry a box.
It gives a perfectly good answer: grip here, keep the load close, watch the balance. A
prompt went in, an answer came out, and the world is exactly as it was. If the answer is
bad you press regenerate. Nothing was spent.

On the right, the same question, but the machine has to actually do it. It senses, it
decides, it acts — and then the thing it just did becomes part of what it senses next.
That arrow along the bottom is the whole difference. Output is force, not words.

If a language model writes a bad paragraph, you delete it. If the robot drops the box,
the box is on the floor, the contents are broken, and every decision after that is made
in the world that mistake created. There is no regenerate button."

---

## 7 — What happens on every pass

"Let me slow the loop down, because the vocabulary matters.

Sensors. Perception — turning raw signal into a usable picture. State — what's true
now plus what happened a moment ago. Policy — the decision-maker. Actuators — the
motors that actually move.

The only jargon worth memorising is *policy*. It means the decision-maker: a function
from what I sense to what I do. When someone says a robot 'learned a policy,' that's
all they mean — the mapping was trained instead of written by hand.

And notice the speed. A driving stack goes round this loop maybe fifty times a second.
A walking robot's balance controller closer to a thousand, because if it's late it's
already falling."

---

## 8 — The easy things turned out to be the hard things

"So why is this hard? Four reasons. Here is the first.

Look at the two lists. Everything on the left — chess, calculus, exams — computers took
from us decades ago. Everything on the right, a four-year-old does without thinking,
and we still can't do reliably.

That inversion has a name: Moravec's paradox. Your hands and eyes have half a billion
years of evolution behind them. Arithmetic has maybe five thousand. The skills that
feel effortless are the ones with the most machinery underneath — which makes them the
ones with the most left to rebuild."

---

## 9 — Small errors snowball

*The most important slide in the deck. Slow down.*

"Second reason, and if you remember one technical idea today, make it this one.

Say you train a driving policy by copying a human. During training the car is always
near the middle of the lane, because the human kept it there. That's the blue band.

Now it drives. It drifts slightly — and now it is somewhere it has never seen, so its
next guess is a little worse, which puts it further out, where the next guess is worse
again. That's the staircase.

In text this barely matters. In control it's the difference between arriving and
crashing. Most of the craft of this field is various ways of breaking that feedback."

---

## 10 — 99% reliable is a failing grade

"Third. Reliability numbers that sound excellent are useless here.

A robot making a thousand decisions a day at ninety-nine per cent accuracy fails ten
times a day. Ten broken items, ten interventions, ten times somebody walks over. That
is not a product.

You need the third bar. And every extra nine costs roughly as much work as all the
nines before it combined. A wet floor. A child's balloon. Low sun through fog. Each one
is rare. Together they are constant."

---

## 11 — There is no internet of robot data

"Fourth: the data doesn't exist.

Language models got a free gift — the entire web, already written and paid for. There
is no equivalent for a robot hand doing four hundred million things. Somebody has to
teleoperate a real machine, hour by hour. That's why data collection is a genuine moat
in robotics in a way it simply isn't in text.

The obvious workaround is simulation. But simulated friction, contact and lighting are
never quite right, so a policy perfect in sim can fail on the real machine. That's the
reality gap. The standard fix is at the bottom: train across thousands of slightly
*wrong* physics settings, so reality looks like one more variation."

---

## 12 — Simulation is the factory floor

*Transition. The next four slides are the tooling section.*

"Which brings us to where the data actually comes from.

One real robot produces one hour of data per hour, needs a human beside it, and breaks
things. One GPU runs thousands of simulated robots at once, overnight, for the price of
electricity.

Four things you get. Parallelism — Isaac Lab runs four thousand-plus worlds on a single
GPU. Safety — it can fall down the stairs for nothing. Free labels — the simulator already
knows the pose of every object, so the ground truth is perfect and nobody annotated it.
And randomisation, which is the main defence against the gap we just talked about.

*Point at the render on the right.*

And look at what that fleet is actually doing. Most of those runs are failures — robots
stumbling, robots face-down, a handful getting to the end. That is not a flaw in the
picture, it is the whole point. Learning to walk means falling over a few million times,
and the only reason that is affordable is that none of those falls cost you a robot."

---

## 13 — The simulation landscape

*Practical slide. Two facts here save people real time.*

"The tools, and two things worth knowing.

MuJoCo is DeepMind's engine and the academic default — easy to install, best-in-class
for contact-rich manipulation, and MJX runs the same physics on GPU.

Isaac Sim is NVIDIA's full simulator: PhysX, photoreal rendering, sensor simulation.

Now the first thing worth knowing: **Isaac Lab is not a simulator.** It's the learning
framework on top of Isaac Sim. And the second: **Isaac Gym is deprecated.** It was the
first GPU RL simulator and it's been folded into Isaac Lab. If someone on your team is
starting there, stop them.

Newton is the interesting newcomer — an open, differentiable engine jointly built by
NVIDIA, Google DeepMind and Disney Research. Rivals sharing a physics layer tells you
something.

Rule of thumb at the bottom: MuJoCo to prototype and evaluate, Isaac Lab when you need
thousands of parallel worlds."

---

## 14 — Where simulation is heading

"Four trends.

GPU-parallel is now the baseline, not a feature. The field is consolidating on shared
standards — OpenUSD for scenes, one open engine underneath. The simulator is becoming a
data factory: the output people want is a dataset, not a demo, and NVIDIA reports
cutting synthetic-data generation from months to about thirty-six hours.

And the fourth is the one to watch. Learned simulators — world models that generate
interactive scenes straight from video. A 2026 survey of two hundred systems found they
already substitute for hand-built simulators in narrow cases, but still lose on
guaranteed physics and long-horizon consistency. Not yet a replacement. Closer than it
was."

---

## 15 — ROS

"The other half of the stack, and the part that isn't glamorous.

ROS is not an operating system. It's a message bus plus an ecosystem: one program
publishes a topic, others subscribe, and nobody needs to know who is listening. Around
it you get drivers for most sensors and arms, MoveIt for motion planning, Nav2 for
navigation, rviz to see what the robot thinks, rosbag to replay a failure.

Two honest caveats. First, version: ROS 1 is over — Noetic reached end of life in May
2025, so ROS 2 is the only maintained line, and Jazzy is the LTS through 2029.

Second, and more important: the fast loop usually runs *outside* ROS. A hundred-hertz
policy will not wait on middleware. Several humanoid stacks use almost none of ROS,
keeping it for hardware I/O and logging."

---

## 16 — Three approaches

"So how are these things actually built? Three generations, top to bottom.

The old way: a pipeline where engineers write and tune every box. Inspectable, and
brittle — every new situation needs a new rule.

The middle way: one big learned network from sensing to action. Handles mess the rules
couldn't, but it's opaque and data-hungry.

The current way: start from a model already trained on internet images and text, then
teach it to output motion. Here's why that matters — it already knows what a mug is.
You don't have to teach it mugs.

That third row is why generative AI and robotics stopped being separate conversations."

---

## 17 — Three jobs, not three technologies

"Let me place all of this on a map, because people mix these terms up constantly.

Three circles, and they're three *jobs*, not three technologies. Perceptual: take the
world in. Generative: make something new. Embodied: act on the world.

The overlaps are where the interesting things live. Perception plus generation is
multimodal — it sees, then describes. Perception plus embodiment is classic robot
sensing, decades old.

And the middle is the frontier: a VLA perceives, generates and acts at once."

---

## 18 — Four model types

"Which brings us to the acronyms, because people use them interchangeably and they are
not the same thing.

LLM: text in, text out. VLM: add eyes. VLA: add hands. World model: add foresight.

The key point is the one at the bottom. Each is the previous one plus a channel.
Nothing is thrown away and rebuilt — it's added to. The next four slides take one each."

---

## 19 — LLM

"The language model is the reasoning backbone.

What it adds is language. It can break 'tidy the kitchen' into steps, argue about the
order, and write code to call the robot's existing skills.

Where it stops: it has never seen the kitchen. With no eyes it can't tell whether the
mug is where it assumed, so the plan is only as good as the description it was handed.

You meet it as the high-level planner above a robot stack — and as the thing the next
three models are built out of."

---

## 20 — VLM

"Add vision and you get grounding.

Now it can say what's on the table, roughly where, whether the drawer is open. The words
are tied to the actual scene.

Where it stops: the output is still language. It can tell you the mug is left of the
kettle. It cannot move an arm twelve centimetres to reach it.

You meet it as the perception-and-reasoning half of nearly every robot policy in use
today. In GR00T, for instance, a vision-language backbone sits frozen inside the model."

---

## 21 — VLA

*The centre of gravity of the technical section.*

"Add the last channel and you get a vision-language-action model.

Camera images, an instruction in plain English, the robot's joint positions in. Joint
targets and gripper commands out. It keeps the web knowledge of the vision-language
model and bolts on an action head — usually diffusion or flow matching.

Where it stops is speed. A big model cannot think at a hundred hertz, so most designs
split it: the heavy model plans at five to ten hertz, and a small action expert runs the
motion at fifty to a hundred.

This is the leading frontier architecture and what people mean by 'robot foundation
model' — though production deployment is still early. RT-2 coined the term in 2023, OpenVLA put an open one in every lab, and π-zero,
GR00T, Gemini Robotics and Helix turned it into production systems."

---

## 22 — World model

"And the fourth: instead of only learning 'see this, do that,' the system learns to
predict what would happen if it acted.

Reach across the top — knocks the glass. Push it to the edge — falls off. Grip and lift
— works. Pick that one.

That's something like imagination: a way to check a plan before spending a real action
on it. Which, if you think back to the snowball slide, is exactly the medicine that
problem needs. It's also how a lot of synthetic training data now gets made."

---

## 23 — How you would actually build one

*The applied slide. Walk the eight boxes quickly; spend the time on steps 2 and 8.*

"So how does this actually enter a robot programme? Eight steps, and the order matters.

Define the task and what counts as success. Then — before any model work — build the
evaluation harness. Everyone does this last. Doing it second is the single highest-value
change you can make, because without it you cannot tell whether anything you do
afterwards helped.

Then collect teleoperation data, somewhere between fifty and a few hundred demonstrations,
and augment it in simulation. Fine-tune an open VLA — π-zero, GR00T, OpenVLA, SmolVLA.
Evaluate in sim, which is cheap and will flatter you. Then hardware in the loop in a
contained cell. Then shadow mode, where the policy proposes and a human commits. Then
deploy with a classical fallback underneath.

And after that, the loop that never ends: log every intervention, turn the failures into
the next dataset, retrain, re-run the same harness so the numbers stay comparable.

One line to take away, at the bottom. Draw the safety boundary early. The learned policy
owns 'what to do next.' It never owns 'do not hit the wall' — that stays in the controller
underneath, where you can still prove it."

---

## 24 — Model labs and the platform

*Pick two of the four to speak to; let people read the rest.*

"A little detail on the brains.

DeepMind, founded 2010 in London, does frontier research generally — robotics is one
arm of it. Their Gemini Robotics family includes a version small enough to run on the
robot itself, and in 2026 they partnered with Boston Dynamics to put it on Atlas.

NVIDIA, founded 1993, is the platform. GR00T N1 in March 2025 was the first open
foundation model for humanoids.

Physical Intelligence is the one to watch on the startup side: founded only in 2024,
and its π-zero models have already become the baseline other labs compare against.
Skild raised close to one and a half billion dollars chasing a model that drives any
robot — with public deployment evidence still thinner than the funding."

---

## 25 — American humanoid makers

"The American bodies.

Boston Dynamics is the oldest name here — 1992, out of MIT, now owned by Hyundai.
Thirty years of setting the hardware benchmark, and now handing the thinking half to
DeepMind.

Figure is the best-funded pure-play in the field at a reported thirty-nine billion
dollar valuation. Builds the body and the Helix model that drives it.

But if you want the one furthest along in *paid* work, it's Agility. Digit is the least
humanoid-looking robot on the slide and it's the one actually moving totes for
customers, with reported contracted orders over three hundred million dollars.

Apptronik came out of a University of Texas lab and sells Apollo as a service, with
Mercedes-Benz and GXO as named partners."

## 26 — Where the units are actually shipping

*The contrast with slide 6 is the point. Say it explicitly.*

"Now compare that with Asia and Europe, because the game is different.

Unitree sets the price floor for the entire industry — the G1 base price has fallen to
around thirteen and a half thousand dollars. AgiBot is the volume leader: roughly five
thousand units shipped in 2025, its ten-thousandth humanoid by March 2026, and it
publishes an open dataset other labs train on.

UBTech is unusually candid — it has described its own robots as no more than half as
efficient as a human worker, which is more honesty than most of this industry offers.

And NEURA in Germany is Europe's standard-bearer, suddenly far ahead of other European
startups on funding.

So: the American companies have the valuations. The Chinese companies have the units."

---

## 27 — Which countries lead, and at what

"That difference shows up in the map.

China has more humanoid companies than anyone — about twenty-one of the forty-five or
so worldwide — and Unitree and AgiBot together are expected to account for close to
eighty per cent of this year's shipments.

The United States has fourteen companies, the largest valuations, and nearly all the
model labs. North America took nearly sixty per cent of physical-AI venture dollars
this year.

Europe has a smaller funding signal but serious factory demand. Japan supply
components and, quietly, ownership — Hyundai owns Boston Dynamics.

One caveat: the counts differ between trackers depending on what qualifies as a
humanoid company. Read the split, not the digits."

---

## 28 — Robots running on these models

"And here is what those companies have actually put on the floor.

Figure builds both the body and the model. Atlas is the clean split — Boston Dynamics
hardware, DeepMind brain. Optimus descends from Tesla's driving stack. Digit is the
warehouse workhorse. Apollo runs an on-device model that adapts from about fifty
demonstrations. And Unitree and AgiBot are the volume story.

The line at the bottom matters more than the list: almost all of this is pilots and
early paid deployments, not fleets running unattended."

## 29 — Humanoids and quadrupeds

*Engineering audiences want this comparison. It is the most concrete slide in the deck
for them.*

"The impact is not the same for the two form factors, and the difference is structural.

Quadrupeds first. Legged locomotion became highly capable before foundation models existed — massively
parallel RL in simulation with domain randomisation, around 2019 to 2022. That policy is
a small network on proprioception running onboard at fifty to two hundred hertz. It is
learned, but it is not a foundation model and it does not need one.

So for a quadruped, embodied AI arrives as a layer above a product that already sells:
semantic missions instead of waypoint lists, VLM inspection and anomaly detection — which
is most of what these robots are actually paid for — plain-language tasking, and
manipulation once you bolt on an arm. The locomotion controller stays separate, small and
fast. Nothing from the big model enters the balance loop.

Humanoids are the opposite. There, the model *is* the product. The whole reason for the
form factor is generality, and generality is exactly what hand-programming cannot give
you. A humanoid without a learned policy is an extremely expensive single-purpose machine,
and a purpose-built machine beats it every time.

And it inherits a harder problem. Balance yields to the same RL as the quadrupeds.
Bimanual dexterous manipulation does not — contact-rich, high DoF, thin tactile data, and
sim-to-real that works badly for contact and deformables. That forces real teleoperated
data, which is why every humanoid company runs a teleop fleet, and why the two-rate split
we saw on the VLA slide exists at all.

So: same technology, opposite risk profile. Quadruped progress is incremental and backed
by revenue. Humanoid progress is discontinuous and backed by funding.

And one thing to keep in your head when you watch the announcements — a lot of what ships
as a humanoid is a wheeled base with a torso and two arms. On a flat warehouse floor, legs
add cost, power draw and failure modes without adding task value."

---

## 30 — The frontiers

"What's actually unsolved.

Legs are close to solved; hands are not — dexterous manipulation needs tactile sensing
that's still expensive and thinly covered by data. Cross-embodiment: one model, many
bodies, still costs far more retraining than the pitch suggests. Long-horizon autonomy:
most demos are seconds long.

Evaluation is the one nobody likes talking about. There is no agreed benchmark. Success
rates come from private task sets, so numbers between labs rarely compare.

Then learned simulators, and the plain economics — a policy has to run onboard inside a
power budget and beat the cost of the person currently doing the job.

Notice that only two of these six are model problems. The rest are data, measurement,
hardware and economics. That's why progress looks slower than the model releases
suggest."

---

## 31 — Generative AI dominates today

*Don't soften this. It buys you credibility for the next slide.*

"And here's the reality check.

Generative AI product revenue this year is around ninety billion dollars. Physical and
embodied AI, around one and a half. A factor of sixty. Two thirds of organisations
already use generative AI for something; almost none of them own a robot.

But look at the top right: nearly fifty billion dollars of venture funding into physical
AI in the first half of this year alone, about four times the previous half-year.

So the honest summary is the box at the bottom. Embodied AI is raising far more than it
sells. That is a bet on the next decade, not a description of this one.

One caveat — these estimates vary by a factor of four or five between research firms.
Trust the ratio, not the digits."

---

## 32 — Rank the evidence

*Practical slide. Deliver it as advice.*

"If you take one habit away, take this one. When someone shows you a robot, ask what
kind of evidence you're looking at.

A vendor video shows a task *can* be demonstrated. A controlled pilot shows it was
tested somewhere real. A paid deployment shows a customer saw enough value to pay. And
multi-site operation with published uptime and intervention data — that's the only rung
that really means anything.

Most of what you'll read is the first one.

On the right is my honest read of 2026: architecture looks settled, deployments cluster
where failure is tolerable, humanoids are mostly pilots, and the sober forecasts put
reliable unattended autonomy somewhere around 2028 to 2030."

---

## 33 — Where your edge actually is

*The slide the whole talk exists for. Slow down and say it plainly.*

"So here is the argument I actually want to make.

Generative AI is loud right now, and a lot of robotics people are being pulled into it —
building agents, fine-tuning chat models, chasing the thing that is on the front page. I
think that is the wrong move for this room, and here is why.

In LLM and agent work you enter the largest and most crowded talent market in software,
and almost nothing you learned about machines counts there. Kinematics, calibration, latency budgets,
safety cases — none of it is priced in. The work is also crowded and largely settled: the
architectures are known, the leaders have the compute, and the differentiation is in
product rather than engineering.

Now look at the other column. In embodied AI the bottleneck is not model quality. It is
data, evaluation and reliability — teleoperation rigs, demonstration quality, system
identification, latency budgets, safety cases, honest test protocols. Read that list
again: it is a description of a robotics engineer. The people flooding into AI cannot do
any of it, and the field cannot ship without it.

And it is early. Capital has arrived, revenue has not, and there is still no accepted
standard for evaluation. Early is exactly when a scarce skill is worth the most.

One thing I want to be precise about, because it is the obvious objection. This is not
'ignore generative AI.' A VLA is a generative model. You have to learn transformers,
fine-tuning and flow matching either way. The question is where you point them: at a
chatbot, where you are one of a million — or at a body, where you are one of very few."

---

## 34 — Four steps, in this order

*Concrete. Read the four headlines; let them read the detail.*

"If you want to be useful in this field inside a month, do these four things in this
order.

One: get a simulator running this week. MuJoCo installs with pip and Playground gives
you tasks the same afternoon. Go to Isaac Lab only when you actually need thousands of
parallel worlds — not before.

Two: run an open VLA end to end. LeRobot with SmolVLA fits on a consumer GPU. Fine-tune
it on something small. The point isn't the result, it's feeling how much data one new
task costs.

Three: collect a hundred demonstrations yourself. Teleoperate a cheap arm or a simulated
one. Nothing teaches faster that data quality dominates model choice.

Four — and I'd argue this is first in importance even if it's fourth in sequence: build
the evaluation harness. Fixed task list, fixed trial count, randomised initial states,
interventions counted. It is the scarcest artefact in this field. Most teams don't have
one, which is exactly why nobody's numbers compare."

## 35 — Signals worth tracking, and noise

*Close the argument. This is the slide people photograph.*

"And how do you anticipate where this goes, without re-reading the news every week.

Watch seven things. Intervention rate and uptime, published per site over months. Cost
per robot-hour against the wage it would replace. Cross-embodiment results — same policy,
new body, little retraining. The hardware price curve; a G1 at thirteen and a half
thousand dollars changes what is worth automating at all. Whether any evaluation standard
gets adopted across labs. On-device latency and watts at fifty to a hundred hertz. And
whether learned simulators reach production.

Ignore the right-hand column. Demo videos, valuations, shipment counts with no
utilisation figure, teleoperated demos presented as autonomy, benchmark scores on a task
suite the same lab designed.

The reason is the line at the bottom. The timing question is not when the models get
better — they will. It's when the last few percent of reliability arrives. Only the
left-hand column measures that."

## 36 — Four things to carry away

"Four things.

One: the loop is the whole difference. Output is force, not words, so errors compound
instead of being regenerated.

Two: embodied sits on top of generative. Not a rival wave — the hardest layer of the same
stack. Learn the models, then point them at a body rather than a chat window.

Three: the bottleneck is your field, not theirs. Data, evaluation and reliability decide
when this ships, and that is robotics work.

Four: judge by deployments, not demos — including your own. Build the harness, count the
interventions, and be early while being early still pays.

Happy to take questions."

## Likely questions, and short answers

**"Isn't this just telling us to chase a different hype cycle?"**
The test is whether the preparation is wasted if the timeline slips. It isn't. Simulation,
evaluation design, data pipelines and control are durable skills that pay off in any
robotics work, embodied AI or not. Compare that with spending two years building agent
wrappers that a model release makes redundant.

**"Embodied AI is raising far more than it sells. Why start now rather than in 2028?"**
Because scarce skills are worth the most before the field standardises, and because the
gap between capital and revenue is precisely a gap in the things you are good at. If you
wait until reliability is solved, the interesting work is done and you are competing on
model skills alone.

**"Why not just do RL on the real robot?"**
Sample efficiency and wear. Millions of episodes is fine in simulation and impossible on
hardware that costs money and breaks. Real-robot RL is mostly used to fine-tune a policy
pretrained elsewhere.

**"Why generative action heads instead of regressing actions?"**
The action distribution is multimodal — several correct ways to grasp the same mug — and
a regression head averages them into something that grasps nothing. Diffusion and flow
matching sample one mode instead of blending all of them.

**"How do you actually evaluate one of these?"**
Badly, and that is slide 29. Private task suites, small trial counts, no agreed protocol.
If someone quotes a success rate, ask how many trials, on what task distribution, and who
chose the tasks.

**"Does ROS 2 give real-time guarantees?"**
It gives the ingredients — DDS with QoS, a real-time-capable executor — not a guarantee.
Genuinely hard real-time sits below ROS in a dedicated controller, which is the same
reason the learned policy usually runs outside it.

**"Is sim-to-real still just domain randomisation?"**
Still the backbone, now with company: system identification to narrow the randomisation
range, real-to-sim reconstruction from scans, and co-training on simulated plus
teleoperated real data.

**"Is China going to win this?"**
They are winning on a different axis — units, price, manufacturing — while the US leads on
capital and frontier models. Whether volume or intelligence is the binding constraint is
genuinely open.
